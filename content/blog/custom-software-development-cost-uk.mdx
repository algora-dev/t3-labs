Custom software in the UK spans everything from focused five-figure builds — roughly £12,000–£30,000 for a simple internal tool that automates one process — through to six-figure business platforms and £250,000+ enterprise systems. Published UK market ranges are broad for a simple reason: the price is driven as much by team structure, delivery model and scope discipline as by the software itself. Two credible companies can quote £40,000 and £110,000 for what sounds like the same application, and neither is necessarily wrong.

That's the part most pricing guides skip. This article covers the current UK market ranges — then explains *why* quotes vary so much, so you can judge the one in front of you rather than just browse price bands.

## How much does custom software development cost in the UK?

Current published UK market guidance, from independent 2026 pricing guides:

| Type of project | Typical market range | What usually drives the cost |
|---|---:|---|
| Simple internal tool (one process, small team) | £12,000 – £30,000 | Narrow workflow, few or no integrations |
| Mid-complexity business application | £25,000 – £90,000 | Multiple user roles, integrations, admin panel |
| Client-facing web application | £40,000 – £110,000 | External users, authentication, branded UX, API backend |
| SaaS product (MVP) | £50,000 – £120,000 | Multi-tenancy, billing, core feature set |
| Complex enterprise platform | £120,000 – £500,000+ | Many integrations, compliance, scale, long timelines |

Sources: [Seven Solvers 2026 UK pricing guide](https://www.sevensolvers.com/blog/how-much-does-custom-software-cost-in-the-uk-2026-honest-pricing-guide) and [Softomate Solutions 2026 UK bespoke software cost guide](https://www.softomatesolutions.com/blog/bespoke-software-cost-uk/). These are external market ranges for professionally managed UK development — **not T3 Labs prices** — and the ranges overlap because categories blur at the edges. UK developer day rates in these guides run around £350–£850.

Read the table once, then stop worrying about it. The useful question is not "what does software cost" — it's why your specific quote says what it says.

## Why are custom software quotes so different?

### Team structure

A conventional agency delivery can involve sales, discovery consultants, a product manager, project manager, UX/UI designer, frontend developer(s), backend developer(s), QA, DevOps and account management. Each role can be genuinely justified — enterprise software is complicated, and specialists exist for reasons.

But every role is also a line on the invoice. A ten-person delivery team with sequential handoffs costs dramatically more than a two-person senior team working the same problem directly, even at identical day rates. The software doesn't know how many people built it.

### Delivery model

Beyond headcount, the *shape* of delivery moves price:

- **Large teams and sequential handoffs** — work moves between specialists, each transition adding coordination, documentation and waiting.
- **Meetings and project management overhead** — necessary at scale, expensive in aggregate.
- **Manual implementation of routine work** — boilerplate, repetitive UI, standard integrations built by hand.
- **Heavy upfront discovery** — weeks of paid analysis before anything gets built.

None of this is a scam. A traditional agency's cost structure reflects how it operates. But it explains why the same brief can price very differently elsewhere.

## Been quoted £50,000 for custom software?

A £50,000 software quote is not automatically unreasonable — but neither does it mean £50,000 is the only sensible way to build what you need.

Before accepting or rejecting it, inspect:

- **Scope** — is everything in the price actually needed in version one?
- **Architecture** — is it proportionate to the problem, or gold-plated?
- **Integrations** — how many, and how modern are the interfaces?
- **Security and testing** — genuinely important; don't cut these blindly.
- **Design** — bespoke brand-level design, or would a clean standard UI do?
- **Project management** — how much of the quote is coordination rather than building?
- **Team size** — who is actually doing the work, and how many of them are there?
- **Contingency** — is padding quoted as fixed cost?
- **Deferrable features** — what could ship later without harming the business outcome?
- **Existing software** — could an off-the-shelf product replace part of the build?
- **AI-assisted development** — is the developer using modern tooling to reduce manual implementation effort, and does the price reflect that?

The goal is to understand the quote, not to assume it's wrong.

## What are you actually paying for in a software quote?

A typical custom software quote contains:

| Cost area | What it covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding the problem | Discovery, requirements | Protects quality — badly understood problems produce wrong software |
| Solution design and architecture | Technical decisions | Protects quality — cheap here is expensive later |
| UX/UI | How people use it | Influenced by delivery model — standard patterns cost far less than bespoke |
| Development | The build itself | The line most affected by how the team works |
| Integrations | Third-party systems | Each modern API integration is meaningful work; legacy ones far more |
| Testing | QA across the system | Protects quality |
| Security | Auth, data handling, review | Protects quality |
| Deployment and handover | Infrastructure, documentation | Often underestimated |
| Project management | Coordination | Influenced by delivery model — more people means more of it |
| Ongoing support | After launch | Typically 15–25% of build cost per year (Softomate guide) |

The honest summary: roughly half of a typical quote protects quality and shouldn't be squeezed. The other half is heavily influenced by *how* the company builds — and that's where legitimately large differences come from.

## Has AI changed what custom software should cost?

Yes, in some projects — but not simply because AI can generate code.

Experienced AI-assisted development can remove a large amount of manual effort from: initial scaffolding, repetitive implementation, refactoring, test generation, documentation, debugging support, standard API integrations, prototyping and routine UI work.

But skilled humans still own requirements, architecture, business logic, security, edge cases, data handling and deployment decisions. AI accelerates the right kind of team; it doesn't replace judgment.

The practical consequence for buyers: it is now a fair question to ask any developer *how* they use AI and whether their delivery model reflects it. ## What should I ask when comparing two software quotes?

1. What exactly is included in the price — and what isn't?
2. Which features are genuinely custom, versus configured or reused?
3. What existing products or libraries are being reused?
4. Who will actually build the software, and how senior are they?
5. How large is the delivery team, and what does each person do?
6. What discovery and project management is included?
7. How are changes in scope handled and priced?
8. What testing and security work is included?
9. Who owns the source code and IP?
10. What are the ongoing costs — hosting, support, changes?
11. How is AI used in the development process, and does it affect the price?
12. Which parts of the project could be simplified without harming the business outcome?

A confident developer will answer these directly. Evasion is itself information.

## Do you need everything in the first version?

Usually, no. The commercially sensible move is to build the smallest version that solves the expensive business problem *properly* — not a throwaway demo, but a focused first version that handles the core workflow end-to-end and defers the nice-to-haves.

Market guidance is consistent here: businesses that try to build everything in version one routinely spend multiples of what was needed (the Seven Solvers guide puts feature-level additions at £2,000–£8,000 each, compounding fast). Cut to the 10–15 things your users actually need, then iterate on real usage.

The failure mode to avoid is the opposite extreme: a "phase one" so thin it solves nothing, forcing a rebuild. Focused ≠ disposable.

## When should you get a second opinion?

Consider it when:

- the quote feels disproportionate to the business problem it solves;
- two quotes differ dramatically and neither explains why;
- the proposed delivery team seems very large for the scope;
- the timeline seems surprisingly long;
- there's substantial paid discovery before anything gets built;
- you suspect existing software could handle part of the problem;
- you want to know whether AI-assisted development changes the approach.

A second opinion isn't about shopping for the cheapest number — it's a sanity check on whether the delivery approach fits the problem.
