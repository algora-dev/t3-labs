> T3 Labs builds custom systems for businesses — so we have every incentive to say "hire us". This article tries to be more useful than that: here are all your options, including the ones that don't involve us.

## The situation

You've had an idea — or AI gave you one. It's a good idea: it would genuinely save time or make money. You can describe it clearly. You just can't build it, and you don't know what route people like you take.

Here are the real options, honestly.

## Option 1: No-code tools

Zapier, Make, Airtable, Notion, and a dozen others let you assemble working systems without writing code. Modern ones have AI features built in.

- **Good when:** your idea is a workflow — "when this happens, do that" — with standard building blocks.
- **Watch out:** you hit a ceiling. The moment your idea needs custom logic, or talks to a system the platform doesn't support, you're stuck — and platforms with per-task pricing get expensive at volume.

## Option 2: Existing SaaS

Someone may have already built your idea as a product. Before building anything, search hard for it. If a £30/month tool does 90% of what you want, that beats a custom build that does 100% at fifty times the cost. We tell clients this regularly; the right answer is often "just buy X".

## Option 3: Automation platforms

If the core of the idea is "connect A to B" — new email becomes a CRM record, form submission triggers a sequence — automation platforms (often with AI steps inside) are the cheapest, fastest route. Many "AI ideas" are really integration ideas with an AI step in the middle.

## Option 4: AI coding tools

Tools like Cursor, Claude Code and Copilot let you build real software by describing what you want. This is genuinely viable — we use these tools professionally.

- **Good when:** you enjoy tinkering, the stakes are low, and you have time to learn.
- **Watch out:** you can get something working quickly and then be unable to fix, secure, or extend it. The 80% is easy; the last 20% — auth, edge cases, deployment, data safety — is where non-developers stall. Fine for an internal experiment; risky for anything your business depends on.

## Option 5: Freelancers / developers

Hiring a freelancer to build it is the traditional route.

- **Good when:** the scope is crisp, you can write a clear spec, and you can evaluate technical work.
- **Watch out:** if you can't yet describe *what* should be built in business terms, you're paying someone to make decisions they're not positioned to make. Spec quality determines outcome quality.

## Option 6: Bespoke implementation

A studio (like us, but not only us) that takes the business problem, decides the technology, builds, tests, deploys and hands over.

- **Good when:** you want somebody else to make the decisions and the implementation practical; the idea is commercially important; or you simply don't want to become a part-time product manager.
- **Watch out:** the most expensive option upfront — which is why the honest providers start by checking whether options 1–3 would solve your problem first.

## How to choose

Three questions:

1. **Is it a workflow or a product?** Workflow → try automation/no-code first. Product → options 4–6.
2. **What happens if it breaks?** Internal convenience tool → cheap routes fine. Business-critical → invest in doing it properly.
3. **Do you want capability or outcome?** Want to learn → option 4. Want it handled → option 5 or 6.

## When not to build at all

If the idea saves twenty minutes a week, none of these routes pay for themselves. If existing software already covers it, buying beats building. Part of our job is telling people this before they spend money.
