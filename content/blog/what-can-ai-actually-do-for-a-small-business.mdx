> T3 Labs builds AI systems for small businesses, including the AI-assisted workflows inside our own product, QuoteCore+. This guide is written from first-hand implementation experience — including the times AI was the wrong answer.

## The honest version

Most "AI for business" content is written to sell AI. This guide is written to answer a simpler question: if you run a small business in the UK, what can AI realistically take off your plate today — and what should stay exactly as it is?

## Where AI works well right now

**Admin and repetitive document work.** If a task involves reading something, extracting information from it, and putting that information somewhere else, AI is genuinely good at it. Invoices, enquiry emails, application forms, supplier documents — the AI reads, the data lands in the right field, and a person checks rather than retypes.

**Handling enquiries.** AI can categorise incoming enquiries, draft replies, answer the repetitive questions customers ask ten times a week, and flag the unusual ones for a human. That's the pattern we run ourselves: our own intake system reads typed and spoken business problems, asks a sensible follow-up question, and produces a structured brief — with a human reviewing what arrives.

**Quoting and estimating.** This is where we have the deepest experience, because we built it. [AI Scan Assist in QuoteCore+](https://quote-core.com/features/ai-scan-assist) reads a roof plan and identifies the planes, ridges, hips, valleys and barges, so the roofer verifies rather than measures from scratch. The lesson generalises: AI is strongest at the extraction and structuring step of quoting — the typing-up step, not the pricing judgment.

**Reporting and summarising.** If someone in your business assembles the same report by hand every week from the same sources, that's automatable. Sometimes with AI, sometimes with plain automation — see below.

**Internal knowledge.** Most small businesses have their operational knowledge spread across people's heads, inboxes and spreadsheets. AI systems can sit on top of that and answer questions in plain English — provided the information is somewhere it can be read.

## Where human judgment still matters

Anything where the cost of being wrong is high, or the rules are commercial rather than technical:

- Final pricing decisions and commercially sensitive figures
- Complaints, disputes and sensitive customer conversations
- Anything with legal or regulatory obligations attached
- Unusual cases that don't match the pattern the AI learned

The right design isn't "no AI" — it's AI doing the bulk of the work with a human checkpoint where judgment matters. AI reads a hundred documents; you check the five it's unsure about. That's not a compromise; that's the system working properly.

## Where ordinary automation beats AI

If a process is fully rule-based — the same input always produces the same output — you don't need AI. A zap, a script, or a scheduled job is cheaper, faster, more reliable and easier to debug. AI earns its place when there's variation: messy inputs, human language, or judgment at the edges. If your "AI idea" is really "when X happens, do Y", build it as automation and keep AI out of it.

## Where existing software beats building anything

Sometimes the honest answer is: this already exists, buy it. Off-the-shelf tools handle bookkeeping, scheduling, CRM, email marketing and plenty more perfectly well. If a £30/month tool solves the problem, building a custom AI system is a waste of your money. We tell clients this when it's true — a consultancy that always recommends building has a conflict of interest.

## Where custom implementation makes sense

Custom work earns its cost when your process is genuinely specific to your business, when existing tools don't fit how you actually work, or when the competitive advantage is the workflow itself. That's why QuoteCore+ exists: generic quoting tools didn't match how roofers actually work. Custom AI and automation makes sense in the same situations — the process is yours, and generic tools can't follow it.
